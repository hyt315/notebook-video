# Opening design: promise, question, evidence

Use this reference when an opening needs deliberate story framing, especially for a short explainer or promotional film. A cold open, open loop, oversized headline, or animated hook is optional; the non-optional task is to orient the viewer without distorting the evidence or delaying the lesson.

## Choose an opening that fits the learning task

Possible starting points include:

- a verified result or concrete consequence;
- a specific misconception or question the video will answer;
- a small worked example, observed failure, or before/after comparison;
- a plain statement of what the viewer will be able to understand or do.

Choose one clear entry point. Use only claims supported by the source material, preserve necessary qualifications, and show a visual that carries the spoken claim. A readable static diagram, example, or line of text is valid; an opening does not need a fixed number of visual changes, a sound cue, or a particular layout.

## Connect the opening to the body

If the opening creates a question or withholds an explanation, identify where the film answers it. Make the callback add evidence, a causal link, or a worked resolution rather than merely repeating the headline. A direct linear explanation does not need an open loop or callback.

A useful storyboard note can record the opening claim/example, the intended viewer question, and the later explanation. Keep those notes proportional to the actual story rather than filling a required template for its own sake.

## Review the opening

- Can a viewer identify the topic or question from the opening at the intended viewing size?
- Does the visual meaningfully support the spoken line, or is it only decorative?
- Are text, contrast, timing, and captions comfortable on the 16:9 mobile preview?
- Does the body actually explain any promise made by the opening?
- Would a simpler still, diagram, or direct statement teach the same idea more clearly?

Do not reject an opening for being static, calm, or direct. Revise it when the evidence, visual carrier, or learning purpose is unclear—not to hit a duration, action count, or retention formula.
